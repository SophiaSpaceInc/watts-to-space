Pointing a radiator at the Sun

The Sun fills almost none of the sky. Here is why it still matters.

A radiator in orbit looks out at a sky that is almost all 3 K. The Sun's disc covers about 0.001 % of what a flat face sees, so turning a face toward the Sun hardly blocks its view of cold space. The instinct that the Sun is a small thing in the sky is right.

It is wrong about the heat. The Sun's surface is at 5,772 K, and because thermal emission goes as T⁴, that tiny patch delivers 1,361 W per m²: about twice what a 60 °C radiator face sends out. How much of it the face keeps depends on its coating. White paint absorbs about a fifth; black paint absorbs nearly all of it.

A companion to watts-to-space, the full model, and to Your laptop in space. The full model keeps its radiators knife-edge to the Sun; this page lets you turn them toward it.

Turn the panel toward the Sun

A flat panel radiating from both faces, held edge-on to Earth below. Sunlight strikes one face at the angle you set; 90° is knife-edge, the way space data centers fly their radiators.

Inputs

Net heat rejected per m² of panel, both faces

What this page leaves out

  • The back face is assumed to be in shade, and the panel is at one temperature everywhere (no fin losses).
  • Earth's reflected sunlight is averaged over a dawn–dusk orbit (β = 70°, like the full model's Starmind case). In a noon–midnight orbit it would be larger, and the panel would also pass through Earth's shadow.
  • The panel stays edge-on to Earth. Turn one face to Earth instead and, at 600 km, that face absorbs about 3.4 times as much from Earth, and the panel as a whole about 1.7 times (its back face then sees only sky). The full model's radiator orientation setting covers that.
