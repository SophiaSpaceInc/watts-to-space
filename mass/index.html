Watts per kilogram

The figure of merit for getting rid of heat in space.

Every kilogram launched costs money, so the useful question about a radiator is how much heat it rejects per kilogram. Two things set the answer. Physics sets the heat per square metre: at 50 °C in low Earth orbit, a white-painted panel radiating from both faces sheds about 1,000 W per m², and only a hotter radiator does better. Engineering sets the mass per square metre: the panel, its tubes and coolant, armour against micrometeoroids and the deployment mechanism, plus pumps and plumbing for every kilowatt it carries. Divide one by the other.

Two things follow. At a given temperature, a lighter radiator is the main lever, until the pumps dominate. And the pumps set a ceiling: with mP kilograms per kilowatt, no radiator, however light, beats 1,000 ÷ mP W/kg.

What this page leaves out

  • The heat path. The radiator's temperature is set by how hot the chips may run and how much temperature the path from chip to radiator spends. The full model's Part 1 covers it.
  • Sizing margin. The lines assume the radiator is exactly as big as the heat needs. The reference cases' own W/kg include their margins and their own pump mass, so a dot can sit near a line it does not quite reach.
  • Sunlight and Earth. The panel is knife-edge to the Sun and edge-on to Earth at 600 km, as in the full model's Starmind case; at 50 °C Earth's warmth costs about 10 %.
  • The power side. This is thermal mass only. The solar array that powers the payload has its own mass, which the full model adds.
